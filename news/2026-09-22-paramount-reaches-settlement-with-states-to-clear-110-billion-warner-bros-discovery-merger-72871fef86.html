Paramount Reaches Settlement with States to Clear $110 Billion Warner Bros. Discovery Merger

Paramount has resolved a legal challenge from California and 11 other states, clearing a major hurdle for its $110 billion acquisition of Warner Bros. Discovery, though the settlement has drawn criticism from antitrust advocates.

Paramount has reached a formal settlement with California and 11 other states, resolving an antitrust lawsuit that sought to block its massive acquisition of Warner Bros. Discovery The Verge. The agreement clears a major hurdle for the corporate combination, though the outcome has drawn swift condemnation from antitrust advocates and Democratic lawmakers Ars Technica.

Details of the Proposed Consent Decree

Under the terms of the proposed consent decree filed with the court, the merged entity must adhere to several structural and behavioral conditions. Paramount is required to meet minimum theatrical film release quotas over a five-year period, committing to 30 releases in years one and two, followed by 32 releases in subsequent years. At least four of these backed films must be independent projects, and a minimum of 20 percent must be classified as tentpole blockbusters carrying budgets of $50 million or more, securing releases on at least 3,000 U.S. screens within their first month.

Should Paramount fail to meet these theatrical requirements, the company faces penalties including a $30 million fine per missed film and the divestment of Miramax Studios. Additional provisions mandate that the combined company continue offering a free streaming service, such as Pluto TV, and maintain a five-year freeze on selling the Paramount Studios and Warner Bros. lots in California. The agreement also establishes rules for cable channel negotiations and sets up a news editorial independence board consisting of five established journalists with at least 10 years of experience to oversee CBS and CNN.

Reactions and Political Context

The settlement was finalized just days before a ticking fee was set to go into effect on September 30th, which would have required Paramount to pay WBD shareholders 25 cents per share—costing roughly $7 million daily—for each quarter the transaction was delayed.

California Attorney General Rob Bonta defended the arrangement at a press conference, asserting that the agreement protects market competition, consumer choice, and the needs of workers The Verge. However, other state attorneys general who initially stood alongside California—including representatives from New York, Massachusetts, Connecticut, and Minnesota—ultimately conceded after concluding that pursuing the expensive litigation without California would be unfeasible, though they successfully pushed for the inclusion of the CBS and CNN editorial boards Ars Technica.

Consumer and media advocacy groups strongly criticized the decision. Free Press co-CEO Jessica J. González expressed disappointment that AG Bonta altered course, while Public Knowledge legal director John Bergmayer argued that the settlement fails to resolve the fundamental issue of diminished studio competition and reduced consumer choice Ars Technica.
